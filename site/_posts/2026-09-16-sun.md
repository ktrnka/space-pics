6 views of the Sun from GOES, SDO, SOHO, ordered from the surface outward: the visible surface, then the hot corona in ultraviolet from two different spacecraft, then the outer corona seen by a coronagraph that blocks the disc.


## HMI continuum on SDO

<img src="{{ '/assets/img/2026-09-16/sdo-20260917_120000_1024_HMIIC.jpg' | absolute_url }}" alt="SDO HMI intensitygram">

<p class="caption">2026-09-17 12:00 UTC<br>
<a href="{{ '/debug/sdo.html' | relative_url }}">Explore this source</a> · <a href="https://sdo.gsfc.nasa.gov/data/">Original</a> · NASA/SDO and the AIA, EVE, and HMI science teams</p>

*HMI continuum* is short for Helioseismic and Magnetic Imager continuum intensitygram. HMI images the full disc at 4096x4096 pixels, roughly 1 arcsecond resolution, updating every 45 seconds. The continuum frame shows the granulated surface and any sunspots but carries none of the magnetic or velocity information HMI is actually built to measure. Dark, sharp-edged spots are sunspots, cooler regions where a strong magnetic field suppresses convection; the mottled texture elsewhere is granulation, the tops of convection cells roughly 1,000 km across. The disc's edge looks dimmer than its centre from limb darkening, not from any real feature.

## AIA, 304 Å on SDO

<img src="{{ '/assets/img/2026-09-16/sdo-20260917_120854_1024_0304.jpg' | absolute_url }}" alt="SDO AIA 304 Å">

<p class="caption">2026-09-17 12:08 UTC · <abbr title="Wavelength of the emission line this channel isolates">304 Å</abbr><br>
<a href="{{ '/debug/sdo.html' | relative_url }}">Explore this source</a> · <a href="https://sdo.gsfc.nasa.gov/data/">Original</a> · NASA/SDO and the AIA, EVE, and HMI science teams</p>

*AIA* is short for Atmospheric Imaging Assembly. Each channel isolates one temperature layer of the solar atmosphere; the colours are false and assigned by convention. 304 Å, He II, about 50,000 K, red: chromosphere and prominences. 171 Å, Fe IX, about 630,000 K, gold: quiet corona and coronal loops. 193 Å, Fe XII (and Fe XXIV in flares), about 1.2 million K: corona and coronal holes. 211 Å, Fe XIV, about 2 million K: active regions. 335 Å, Fe XVI, about 2.5 million K: active regions. 94 Å, Fe XVIII, about 6 million K: flaring plasma. 131 Å, Fe VIII plus Fe XXI/XXIII, roughly 400,000 K to 16 million K: flares across a wide temperature range. 1600 Å, C IV plus continuum, about 100,000 K and 5,000 K: transition region and photosphere. 1700 Å, continuum, about 5,000 K: photosphere. Native frames are 4096x4096 pixels at 0.6 arcsec/pixel; this site uses NASA's 1024 px browse copies. A note on the labels: 'Fe XIV' is spectroscopists' shorthand for an iron atom that has lost 13 of its electrons (I is neutral, II has lost one, and so on). Stripping that many electrons takes a temperature of about 2 million K, and each ion glows at its own set of wavelengths, so tuning a channel to the 211 Å line of Fe XIV is a way of photographing only the gas at that temperature. That is why each AIA channel is really a temperature map, and why the same active region looks different from channel to channel. Dark patches in 193 or 211 are coronal holes (open field, source of fast solar wind); bright tangled patches are active regions over sunspots; dark thread-like filaments on the disc in 304 are the same structures seen as bright prominences when they curve above the limb. A sudden bright flash in 131 or 94 is a flare.

## SUVI, 195 Å on GOES-19

<img src="{{ '/assets/img/2026-09-16/helioviewer-2003-20260917T115902.png' | absolute_url }}" alt="GOES SUVI 195 Å">

<p class="caption">2026-09-17 11:59 UTC<br>
<a href="{{ '/debug/helioviewer.html' | relative_url }}">Explore this source</a> · <a href="https://helioviewer.org/?date=2026-09-17T11:59:02Z">Original</a> · Helioviewer.org; data courtesy of the respective instrument teams</p>

*SUVI* is short for Solar Ultraviolet Imager. SUVI's six channels, 94, 131, 171, 195, 284 and 304 Å, sample much the same ions and temperatures as SDO/AIA and SOHO/EIT: about 630,000 K in 171 Å, several million K in 94 and 131 Å, and the cooler chromosphere in 304 Å. Its design and role closely mirror EIT's. SUVI's 195 Å channel and SDO AIA's 193 Å channel both isolate the same ion, Fe XII at about 1.2 million K, so the two should show the same coronal structures from nearly the same vantage; differences are mostly resolution (SUVI is a much smaller telescope) and processing. That makes SUVI a good sanity check on AIA, and a fallback when SDO data is late. Read it like AIA or EIT at a matching wavelength: dark patches are coronal holes, bright knots are active regions, sudden brightening is a flare. Frames on this site are normally within minutes of real time.

## AIA 211/193/171 composite on SDO

<img src="{{ '/assets/img/2026-09-16/sdo-20260917_121645_1024_211193171.jpg' | absolute_url }}" alt="SDO AIA 211/193/171 composite">

<p class="caption">2026-09-17 12:16 UTC<br>
<a href="{{ '/debug/sdo.html' | relative_url }}">Explore this source</a> · <a href="https://sdo.gsfc.nasa.gov/data/">Original</a> · NASA/SDO and the AIA, EVE, and HMI science teams</p>

By standard convention, 211 Å (Fe XIV, about 2 million K) is mapped to red, 193 Å (Fe XII, about 1.2 million K) to green, and 171 Å (Fe IX, about 630,000 K) to blue, then combined. Corona bright in all three channels looks golden-white; structures bright mainly in the cooler 171 Å channel look blue; hotter, more disturbed plasma trends toward orange and red. Dark, blue-tinted patches are coronal holes; bright orange-white knots are active regions; fine looping strands are coronal loops tracing magnetic field lines. Colour balance can shift slightly frame to frame since the three source channels are processed independently.

## LASCO, C2 on SOHO

<img src="{{ '/assets/img/2026-09-16/helioviewer-4-20260917T120033.png' | absolute_url }}" alt="SOHO LASCO C2 white-light">

<p class="caption">2026-09-17 12:00 UTC<br>
<a href="{{ '/debug/helioviewer.html' | relative_url }}">Explore this source</a> · <a href="https://helioviewer.org/?date=2026-09-17T12:00:33Z">Original</a> · Helioviewer.org; data courtesy of the respective instrument teams</p>

*LASCO* is short for Large Angle and Spectrometric Coronagraph. Both are white-light cameras recording sunlight scattered by free electrons in the corona (Thomson scattering), not any particular wavelength. C2's occulting disc blocks the inner 1.5 solar radii and images out to about 6 solar radii; C3's disc blocks out to about 3.7 solar radii and images out to about 30 solar radii, a much wider but coarser view. A coronagraph hides the Sun's disc behind an occulting disc (the dark circle) to show the faint outer corona: C2 covers about 2 to 6 solar radii, C3 about 4 to 30. Nothing here maps directly onto the disc images above; the connection is in time. An eruption seen in AIA 304 or 171 on the disc becomes a coronal mass ejection expanding through the LASCO field a few hours later, and streamers here trace the magnetic structures rooted in the active regions above. The white circle marks the Sun's actual size. The dark disc at the centre is the occulting mask, not the Sun (a small marker circle usually shows the Sun's true size and position). A bright, expanding cloud or arc moving outward across a sequence of frames is a coronal mass ejection; faint radial streaks are the ordinary streamer belt; bright dots crossing the field are usually background stars, planets, or sungrazing comets. LASCO frames on this site are normally within minutes of real time.

[LASCO on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_Angle_and_Spectrometric_Coronagraph) · [SOHO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_and_Heliospheric_Observatory)
