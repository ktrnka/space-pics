5 views of the Sun from GOES, SDO, ordered from the surface outward: the visible surface, then the hot corona in ultraviolet from two different spacecraft, then the outer corona seen by a coronagraph that blocks the disc.


## HMI continuum on SDO

<img src="{{ '/assets/img/2026-10-22/sdo-20261022_120000_1024_HMIIC.jpg' | absolute_url }}" alt="SDO HMI intensitygram">

<p class="caption">2026-10-22 12:00 UTC<br>
<a href="{{ '/debug/sdo.html' | relative_url }}">Explore this source</a> · <a href="https://sdo.gsfc.nasa.gov/data/">Original</a> · NASA/SDO and the AIA, EVE, and HMI science teams</p>

*HMI continuum* is short for Helioseismic and Magnetic Imager continuum intensitygram. HMI images the full disc at 4096x4096 pixels, roughly 1 arcsecond resolution, updating every 45 seconds. The continuum frame shows the granulated surface and any sunspots but carries none of the magnetic or velocity information HMI is actually built to measure. A note on the name: HMI takes several images ("filtergrams") at slightly different points across that 617.3 nm iron line's profile. Combining the ones taken away from the line's centre — its "continuum," where the gas isn't doing much special absorbing — gives an ordinary brightness image like this one. Combining the ones nearer the line's centre, where the line shifts and splits in response to motion and magnetic fields, is what yields HMI's dopplergrams and magnetograms instead.
Dark, sharp-edged spots are sunspots, cooler regions where a strong magnetic field suppresses convection; the mottled texture elsewhere is granulation, the tops of convection cells roughly 1,000 km across. The disc's edge looks dimmer than its centre from limb darkening, not from any real feature.

## AIA, 304 Å on SDO

<img src="{{ '/assets/img/2026-10-22/sdo-20261022_120830_1024_0304.jpg' | absolute_url }}" alt="SDO AIA 304 Å">

<p class="caption">2026-10-22 12:08 UTC · <abbr title="Wavelength of the emission line this channel isolates">304 Å</abbr><br>
<a href="{{ '/debug/sdo.html' | relative_url }}">Explore this source</a> · <a href="https://sdo.gsfc.nasa.gov/data/">Original</a> · NASA/SDO and the AIA, EVE, and HMI science teams</p>

*AIA* is short for Atmospheric Imaging Assembly. Each channel isolates one temperature layer of the solar atmosphere; the colors are false and assigned by convention. 304 Å, He II, about 50,000 K, red: chromosphere and prominences. 171 Å, Fe IX, about 630,000 K, gold: quiet corona and coronal loops. 193 Å, Fe XII (and Fe XXIV in flares), about 1.2 million K: corona and coronal holes. 211 Å, Fe XIV, about 2 million K: active regions. 335 Å, Fe XVI, about 2.5 million K: active regions. 94 Å, Fe XVIII, about 6 million K: flaring plasma. 131 Å, Fe VIII plus Fe XXI/XXIII, roughly 400,000 K to 16 million K: flares across a wide temperature range. 1600 Å, C IV plus continuum, about 100,000 K and 5,000 K: transition region and photosphere. 1700 Å, continuum, about 5,000 K: photosphere. Native frames are 4096x4096 pixels at 0.6 arcsec/pixel; this site uses NASA's 1024 px browse copies. A note on the labels: 'Fe XIV' is spectroscopists' shorthand for an iron atom that has lost 13 of its electrons (I is neutral, II has lost one, and so on). Stripping that many electrons takes a temperature of about 2 million K, and each ion glows at its own set of wavelengths, so tuning a channel to the 211 Å line of Fe XIV is a way of photographing only the gas at that temperature. That is why each AIA channel is really a temperature map, and why the same active region looks different from channel to channel. Dark patches in 193 or 211 are coronal holes (open field, source of fast solar wind); bright tangled patches are active regions over sunspots; dark thread-like filaments on the disc in 304 are the same structures seen as bright prominences when they curve above the limb. A sudden bright flash in 131 or 94 is a flare.

## SUVI, 195 Å on GOES-19

<img src="{{ '/assets/img/2026-10-22/helioviewer-2003-20261022T120207.png' | absolute_url }}" alt="GOES SUVI 195 Å">

<p class="caption">2026-10-22 12:02 UTC<br>
<a href="{{ '/debug/helioviewer.html' | relative_url }}">Explore this source</a> · <a href="https://helioviewer.org/?date=2026-10-22T12:02:07Z">Original</a> · NOAA GOES-R, via Helioviewer.org</p>

*SUVI* is short for Solar Ultraviolet Imager. SUVI's six channels are chosen to match AIA's: 304 Å, He II, about 50,000 K: chromosphere and prominences. 171 Å, Fe IX, about 630,000 K: quiet corona. 195 Å, Fe XII, about 1.2 to 1.5 million K: general corona (AIA's equivalent channel sits a couple of ångströms away at 193 Å, but isolates the same ion). 284 Å, Fe XV, about 2 million K: hotter active-region plasma. 131 Å, Fe VIII plus Fe XXI/XXIII, roughly 400,000 K to 16 million K: flares across a wide temperature range. 94 Å, Fe XVIII, about 6 million K: flaring plasma. Its design and role closely mirror AIA's, at lower spatial resolution since SUVI is a much smaller telescope. SUVI's 195 Å channel and SDO AIA's 193 Å channel both isolate the same ion, Fe XII at about 1.2 million K. As with AIA, the Roman numeral after the element counts electrons stripped away, and a given ion always glows at the same wavelength no matter which telescope is looking, which is exactly what lets two different instruments cross-check each other at matching channels. So the two should show the same coronal structures from nearly the same vantage; differences are mostly resolution (SUVI is a much smaller telescope) and processing. That makes SUVI a good sanity check on AIA, and a fallback when SDO data is late. Read it like AIA or EIT at a matching wavelength: dark patches are coronal holes, bright knots are active regions, sudden brightening is a flare. Frames on this site are normally within minutes of real time.

## AIA 211/193/171 composite on SDO

<img src="{{ '/assets/img/2026-10-22/sdo-20261022_121733_1024_211193171.jpg' | absolute_url }}" alt="SDO AIA 211/193/171 composite">

<p class="caption">2026-10-22 12:17 UTC<br>
<a href="{{ '/debug/sdo.html' | relative_url }}">Explore this source</a> · <a href="https://sdo.gsfc.nasa.gov/data/">Original</a> · NASA/SDO and the AIA, EVE, and HMI science teams</p>

By standard convention, 211 Å (Fe XIV, about 2 million K) is mapped to red, 193 Å (Fe XII, about 1.2 million K) to green, and 171 Å (Fe IX, about 630,000 K) to blue, then combined. Corona bright in all three channels looks golden-white; structures bright mainly in the cooler 171 Å channel look blue; hotter, more disturbed plasma trends toward orange and red. Dark, blue-tinted patches are coronal holes; bright orange-white knots are active regions; fine looping strands are coronal loops tracing magnetic field lines. Color balance can shift slightly frame to frame since the three source channels are processed independently.
