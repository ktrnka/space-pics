## ABI GeoColor on GOES-19

<a href="{{ '/debug/goes.html' | relative_url }}">Explore this source</a> · <a href="https://www.star.nesdis.noaa.gov/goes/fulldisk.php?sat=G19">Original</a> · NOAA/NESDIS/STAR</p>

*ABI GeoColor* is short for Advanced Baseline Imager, GeoColor product. A full disc of Earth every 10 minutes. ABI has no true green channel, so daytime GeoColor imagery synthesizes green from the red visible band, a near-infrared band, and blue, giving a true-color-like image at about 0.5-1 km per pixel. At night the same product switches to infrared cloud and fog data, with a fixed, pre-rendered city-lights layer (derived from separate satellite data, not live at the time of the shot) blended in over land. Daytime frames are a true-color-style composite, not raw sensor output. After dark, anything that looks like city lights is a static background layer, not a live detector reading, so it never flickers, changes with local power outages, or lines up exactly with clouds passing overhead; only the cloud and storm structure is current. Watch the terminator (the day/night line) sweep across the disc over the course of a day as GOES-19 holds a fixed spot above the equator.

## EPIC on DSCOVR

<img src="{{ '/assets/img/2026-09-24/epic-20260922150053.jpg' | absolute_url }}" alt="Earth from DSCOVR, centred on 5.9°N 46.8°W">

<a href="{{ '/debug/epic.html' | relative_url }}">Explore this source</a> · <a href="https://epic.gsfc.nasa.gov/">Original</a> · NASA/NOAA EPIC, DSCOVR</p>

*EPIC* is short for Earth Polychromatic Imaging Camera. The full, sunlit disc of Earth, roughly a full hemisphere at a time. The camera actually captures ten separate monochrome exposures, one per filter; the "natural color" image combines three of them (approximately 443 nm blue, 551 nm green, and 680 nm red) into an RGB composite. Onboard averaging brings the native 2048x2048 frame down to about 1024x1024 pixels, roughly 25 km per pixel. New images arrive about every two hours. You are looking at the entire daylit half of Earth at once, always centered near the subsolar point, so the terminator (day/night line) never appears and the perspective barely changes day to day. Because the three color channels are captured seconds apart as Earth rotates underneath, fast-moving clouds can show faint red or blue fringing at their edges. From L1, DSCOVR sees the Moon's far side (the side never visible from Earth) whenever the Moon happens to pass between the camera and Earth, appearing as a small dark silhouette transiting the planet.
